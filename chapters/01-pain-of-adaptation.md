## Love it {#love-it}

In mid-2010, I joined a newly founded start-up in IT project management. We were full of drive and idealism and wanted to work completely differently from what we had experienced in consulting before.

At first, we had no office and only one practical principle: "We have a strategic plan. It's called doing things." We experimented, wrote, learned, and connected with like-minded people.

Adapting to change starts by accepting reality as it is, not as we wish it to be. In leadership, this means embracing constraints and possibilities with clarity.[^love]

When we choose to love the challenge, we unlock agency instead of resistance.

## Leave it {#leave-it}

As the company grew, difficult questions about purpose and identity became unavoidable. I remained convinced that profit alone cannot be the purpose of an organization.

I eventually moved to BMW Group IT and experienced the opposite extreme: from maximum freedom to maximum regulation. The organization was highly structured, and I often felt overprotected and constrained.

At one point, from the "love it, leave it, change it" options, only "leave it" seemed realistic. But that path turned out to be an attempt to move away from discomfort rather than move toward growth.[^obstacle]

Letting go is not weakness. It is disciplined focus.

## Change it {#change-it}

I began engaging in open internal communities, questioned outdated patterns, and joined grassroots change movements such as Working Out Loud.[^wol]

Over time, this led to broader influence around agility and leadership. During the BMW Group IT transformation years, the question of leadership in self-organizing systems became central and eventually led to the six theses behind the Manifesto for Humane Leadership.

What once felt like setbacks became a coherent path in hindsight: "the obstacle became the way." Where improvement is possible, we change conditions rather than blaming individuals.
